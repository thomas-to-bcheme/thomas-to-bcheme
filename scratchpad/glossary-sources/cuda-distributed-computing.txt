## Frame: cuda for deep learning - chp 10 - distributed computing

Distributed computing
This chapter covers
Mapping multi-GPU interconnects and understanding NCCL collectives
Implementing tensor parallelism across 8–16 GPUs
Building pipeline parallel schedules that keep every stage busy
Coordinating tensor and pipeline parallelism across nodes
Evaluating scaling behavior and communication trade-offs
---
The two main ways GPUs communicate inside a server are over the PCIe bus and
NVLink.
---
PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express): This is the standard bus
that connects everything on a motherboard—your GPU, network cards, storage
drives—to the CPU. It’s a general-purpose interconnect. Think of it like the main
city roads; they get you anywhere, but they’re shared and can have traffic jams.
---
NVLink: This is a proprietary, point-to-point interconnect developed by NVIDIA
specifically for connecting GPUs to each other and to the CPU. Think of it as a
private, multi-lane superhighway built exclusively for GPU traffic. It offers
significantly higher bandwidth and lower latency than PCIe.
---
NCCL (NVIDIA Collective Communications Library, pronounced "nickel") is to multi-
GPU programming what cuBLAS is to matrix multiplication: a highly optimized
library that handles the hard parts for you.
---
NCCL provides collective operations that work across multiple GPUs, called
"collective" because all GPUs participate simultaneously.
---
The key insight about NCCL is topology awareness. When you call any collective
operation, NCCL doesn’t just blindly send data around. It looks at your hardware
topology (which GPUs have NVLink, which don’t) and chooses the fastest route.
On a system with NVLink, NCCL will use ring or tree algorithms that maximize
NVLink bandwidth. On systems without NVLink, it falls back to PCIe.
---
Latency: Requires 2(P-1) steps for AllReduce, where P is the number of GPUs
---
Bandwidth optimal: All links active simultaneously, achieving aggregate bandwidth
of P × link_bandwidth
---
10.1.4 Ring versus tree algorithms
---
NCCL implements collective operations using two main algorithmic patterns: ring and tree
---
Ring algorithm
---
The ring algorithm arranges GPUs in a logical ring. Data flows in one direction
around the ring, with each GPU receiving from its predecessor and sending to its
successor. This pattern achieves optimal bandwidth utilization because all links
are active simultaneously.
---
Characteristics:
---
Best for: Large messages (>1 MB), bandwidth-bound operations
---
Used by: AllReduce, ReduceScatter, AllGather
---
Tree algorithm
---
The tree algorithm arranges GPUs in a logical binary tree. During reduction, data
flows up from leaves to root: each leaf sends its value to a parent node, which
sums the inputs and passes the result one level higher. Figure 10.9 shows this
process with four GPUs. Values [1], [2], [3], [4] combine into partial sums at
the intermediate nodes, and the root ends up with the total.
---
Characteristics:
---
Latency optimal: Completes in 2×log(P) steps for AllReduce
---
Bandwidth limited: Not all links active simultaneously
---
Best for: Small messages (<1 MB), latency-bound operations
---
Used by: Broadcast, Reduce, small AllReduce
---
NCCL’s adaptive selection
---
NCCL automatically chooses between ring and tree based on message size and
topology. For AllReduce:
---
Messages < 256 KB: Tree algorithm (latency-optimal)
---
Messages > 1 MB: Ring algorithm (bandwidth-optimal)
---
Messages 256 KB - 1 MB: Hybrid approach
---
This is why you should always use NCCL instead of implementing your own
collectives. NCCL’s decision logic accounts for dozens of factors: NVLink
topology, PCIe switches, message size, data type, and even GPU architecture.
The library has been tuned by NVIDIA engineers over years to extract maximum
performance from every hardware configuration.
---
The communication cost is O(N) per GPU, where N is the size of the data being
reduced. For a model with 1 billion parameters, that’s 4 GB of data at FP32
precision. With NCCL’s ring algorithm, this completes in 2(P-1) communication
steps for P GPUs, which on an 8-GPU system with NVLink takes about 7-10
milliseconds.
---
AllGather is the opposite of ReduceScatter and the second half of AllReduce.
Each GPU starts with a unique piece of data, and AllGather collects all the
pieces and gives the complete set to every GPU. No reduction, no math, just
collecting and distributing.
---
NCCL provides six core collective operations: AllReduce, Broadcast, Gather,
Reduce, ReduceScatter, and AllGather.
---
AllReduce is the workhorse of distributed training, and understanding it is key to
understanding all distributed deep learning. The name tells you exactly what it
does: it reduces (combines) data from all GPUs and then gives the result to all
GPUs.
---
Here’s the concrete scenario: You have 4 GPUs, and each GPU has computed a
partial result. Maybe GPU 0 computed gradients from its batch of training data
and got the value 1. GPU 1 got 2, GPU 2 got 3, and GPU 3 got 4. You want to
combine these partial results by summing them (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10) and then give
that final result (10) to every GPU. That’s exactly what AllReduce does.
---
The "reduce" part means we’re applying an operation to combine the values. The
most common operation is sum, but you can also use max, min, or product. The
"all" part means every GPU gets the final result. This is different from a regular
reduce operation where only one GPU would get the answer.
---
Why is this so important? In data parallel training, each GPU processes a
different batch of data and computes gradients. But you want all GPUs to
update their weights using the average gradient across all batches. AllReduce
with sum gives you the total gradient, which you then divide by the number of
GPUs to get the average. Every GPU performs this AllReduce after its backward
pass, and then every GPU has the same averaged gradients to update its local
copy of the model.
---
In tensor parallelism, we saw this in action: each GPU computed a partial matrix
multiplication result, and AllReduce summed them to give every GPU the complete
output matrix. In distributed batch normalization, you’d use AllReduce to
compute the mean and variance across all GPUs' data, not just one GPU’s batch.
---
Broadcast
---
Broadcast is simpler than AllReduce. One GPU has some data, and you want to
copy that exact data to every other GPU. No combining, no reduction, just
copying.
---
Gather
---
Gather is the opposite of broadcast. Instead of one GPU sending data to
everyone, every GPU sends data to one destination GPU. The destination
collects all the pieces and concatenates them together.
---
Reduce
---
Reduce is like Gather, but instead of just concatenating the pieces, it combines
them with an operation. Think of it as Gather with math applied.
---
ReduceScatter
---
ReduceScatter is more complex, but it’s crucial for understanding modern memory-
efficient training like Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP). The name describes
exactly what happens: reduce the data, then scatter the result.
