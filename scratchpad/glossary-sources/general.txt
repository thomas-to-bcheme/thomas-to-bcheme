## Frame: Communication Frameworks, Methods, and Techniques

Must Signal: I have done ___ which resulted in ___

Key Interview Questions & Their Hidden Meanings:
Tell me about yourself (1:00): Don't just recite your resume. Link your background directly to how you can solve the problem the employer is hiring to fix.

Why did you leave your last job? (2:06): 
Employers are assessing risk. They want to know if you are a flight risk or if you tend to run away from challenges.

Explain your job gap (3:19): 
They want to know if there is a valid reason and, more importantly, how you stayed sharp and relevant during that time.

What’s your greatest weakness? (4:12): 
This tests your self-awareness. Pick a genuine, non-critical weakness and show how you are working to overcome it.

What are your salary expectations? (5:04): 
A game of "chicken." Be honest and provide a market-researched range to avoid wasting time.

Why do you want this job? (6:02): 
They need to see genuine passion and mission rather than just a desire for a paycheck, which could signal you're a flight risk.

Where do you see yourself in 5 years? (6:41): 
A trick question to check for career alignment—does this role realistically lead to your goals, or are you just a temporary filler?

Why should we hire you? (7:50): 
Your final chance to demonstrate clear fit and present information that hasn't been covered yet.

Do you have any other offers? (8:24): 
They need to know if they need to expedite their internal process to stay competitive for you.

Do you have any questions for us? (9:41): 
This is for you to evaluate if the company is a good fit for you. Treat the interview as a two-way street.

## Frame: Engineering Fundamentals & Career — Senior-Level Best Practices

- Novel-problem approach (beyond Neetcode pattern-matching): restate the problem in your own words, hand-trace a small concrete example before coding, then name the technique out loud (decomposition, transformation, search/prune,
greedy, DP) instead of guessing at a memorized template.
- Narrate the brute force first, before optimizing — interviewers grade the reasoning process, not just the final code. Silence reads as stuck; a wrong idea stated and self-corrected reads as senior.
- Practice articulating complexity tradeoffs out loud, not just writing Big-O: time vs. space, average vs. worst case, amortized cost of a resize, and when a worse asymptotic complexity is the right call (small N, cache locality, simpler
code to maintain).
- Advanced git — interactive rebase: squash/reorder/edit commits before opening a PR so history reads as a narrative, not a diary of fixes.
  - `git rebase -i HEAD~n`, then pick/squash/fixup/reword each commit
  - `git rebase --abort` to bail out safely if it goes sideways
- Advanced git — `git bisect` to binary-search a regression: `git bisect start`, mark commits `good`/`bad`, let it walk the range — or automate the whole search with `git bisect run <test-script>`.
- Advanced git — resolving real conflicts: read the `<<<<<<< HEAD` / `=======` / `>>>>>>>` markers as "mine vs. theirs," prefer re-applying intent over blindly picking a side, and use `git rebase --onto` to salvage a branch after a
botched merge.
- Advanced git — hooks (`pre-commit`, `pre-push`) for local automation: gate lint/format/tests before code ever reaches CI or review. Hooks live in `.git/hooks/`, or are shared across a team via a tool like Husky or the `pre-commit`
framework.
- Concurrency fundamentals: a race condition is unsynchronized access to shared mutable state; mutex (exclusive access) vs. semaphore (bounded count) vs. read-write lock; deadlock requires mutual exclusion + hold-and-wait + no
preemption + circular wait — breaking any one prevents it.
- Async patterns: the event loop and non-blocking I/O vs. OS threads; callback to Promise to async/await is a readability evolution, not a performance one; backpressure — a slow consumer signaling a fast producer to slow down — is
the concept most engineers skip until it breaks in production.
- Distributed systems basics: CAP theorem (pick 2 of consistency/availability/partition-tolerance during a network partition); idempotency keys so retries are safe to replay; eventual consistency and read-your-own-writes; exponential
backoff with jitter, not fixed-interval retries.
- Code review culture, giving feedback: comment on the diff, not the author; explicitly label blocking vs. nit vs. question; approve with comments when nothing is blocking so the change isn't stalled waiting on you.
- Code review culture, receiving feedback: assume good intent, ask a clarifying question before pushing back, and thank the reviewer even when you disagree — the goal is a better system, not winning the thread.
- Mentoring: ask questions that lead someone to the answer rather than handing it over; pair on the hardest 20% of a task and let them drive; assign stretch work slightly outside their comfort zone with a safety net in place.
- Technical writing / RFCs: problem statement -> constraints -> alternatives considered (with why each was rejected) -> proposed approach -> rollout/rollback plan. Write for the reader who opens the doc six months from now with
zero context.
- Cross-team communication: over-communicate context before asking for help, default to async written updates over pulling people into meetings, and flag scope or timeline changes the moment you know — never let a surprise land at
the deadline.
- Managing ambiguity is itself a senior skill: when scope is unclear, propose a concrete interpretation and success metric rather than waiting to be told, and timebox exploration/spikes. Senior criteria in practice = influence beyond your
own PRs, sound judgment under uncertainty, and ownership of outcomes rather than just tasks completed.

## Frame: Engineering Fundamentals & Career — Community Resources & Further Reading

- Pro Git (Scott Chacon & Ben Straub) — free official book at git-scm.com/book; the canonical deep dive on rebasing, bisect, and git internals beyond daily-driver usage.
- Learn Git Branching (learngitbranching.js.org) — interactive visual sandbox for practicing interactive rebase, cherry-pick, and merge-conflict resolution without risking a real repo.
- Google Engineering Practices (google.github.io/eng-practices) — official "How to do a code review" and "The CL Author's Guide," the most widely cited public standard for review culture.
- Staff Engineer archetypes & guides (staffeng.com) — Will Larson's site on staff-plus scope and influence: Tech Lead, Architect, Solver, and Right Hand archetypes.
- The Pragmatic Engineer (newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com) — Gergely Orosz's newsletter on career progression and real-world engineering practice at senior/staff level.
- levels.fyi — crowdsourced leveling and compensation data across companies; useful for calibrating what "senior" actually means from one company's ladder to another's.
- Blind (teamblind.com) — anonymous, verified-employee community for compensation, interview-loop, and leveling discussions; read critically, since it's unmoderated.
- Tech Interview Handbook (techinterviewhandbook.org) — free, community-maintained guide covering behavioral prep, negotiation, and the end-to-end interview process, not just algorithms.
- Pramp, now part of Exponent (pramp.com) — free peer-to-peer mock interview practice for rehearsing live problem-solving and communication under time pressure.
