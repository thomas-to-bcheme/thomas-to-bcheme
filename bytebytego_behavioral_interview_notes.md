## 1. Introduction — Why Your Stories Matter as Much as Your Skills
`.../introduction` (no subheadings — paragraph numbers count from the top)

**Main takeaways**
- Technical bar is now standardized and easy to drill for, so behavioral interviews have become the real differentiator between similarly-skilled candidates. (¶6–7)
- Named company values (Google "Googleyness," Meta "Move Fast" / "Build Social Value," Microsoft "Growth Mindset") are really testing: ambiguity tolerance, influence without authority, learning from failure, and raising the effectiveness of people around you. (¶8–9)
- Behavioral performance doesn't just decide yes/no — it decides **level**, and level swings are large: example given is $100K+/yr between mid and senior, with 18–36 months typical to work back out of a down-level. (¶11)
- Down-leveling compounds beyond pay: shrunken scope, less interesting work routed to others, and a motivation hit that makes the eventual promotion harder to earn. (¶12)
- At senior+ levels, behavioral competencies can outweigh raw coding difficulty in leveling decisions — delivering impact *through and with* other people matters as much as technical skill. (¶13)
- "Tell me about a time..." questions test whether you can actually operate under real-world ambiguity — interviewers are watching how you work and decide, not just the final outcome. (¶14)
- The format resists faking: it requires reflecting on real situations and communicating them clearly — which is also just what makes you better at the job itself. (¶15–16)

**Questions/prompts introduced:** none — this chapter is framing/motivation only.

## 2. 01 — The Behavioral Interview Roadmap
`.../the-behavioral-interview-roadmap`

**Book structure** (¶ under intro)
- Part I (Framework): how to find/structure/deliver stories, ends with the Essential Questions.
- Part II (Competencies): specific behaviors companies assess (problem-solving → leadership → innovation), with level-appropriate examples and cultural variation per chapter.
- Closes with "Nailing the Interview" (delivery/handling curveballs) + an afterword.

**The Three Pillars of Preparation** (§"The Three Pillars of Preparation")
1. **Building Your Stories** — turning raw experience into structured stories.
2. **Ensuring Story Strength** — calibrating stories to the *level* you're targeting.
3. **Matching Company Values** — aligning story content to what your target company specifically rewards.
- All three are required together — great stories at the wrong level, or right level but off-values, both fail. (¶ last, "These elements work together")

**How many stories do you need** (§"How Many Stories Do You Need?")
- **Essential Questions (3–4 stories):** every company asks some version of these — see Ch.4 questions below (Tell me about yourself / proud-of project / why this company / weaknesses / failures / challenges). (¶3)
- **Company-specific needs (4–10 stories):** which of the 9 Part-II competencies matter depends on company type:
  - *Startups / high-growth:* Initiative, Delivery, Innovation, Learning (+ maybe Customer Focus). (¶5)
  - *Large tech:* Problem Solving, Cross-team Leadership, Trust & Conflict as core; often also Strategic Leadership, Innovation, Developing Others for senior roles. (¶6)
  - *Traditional enterprise:* Delivery, Trust, Customer Focus, Developing Others; Strategic Leadership for transformation-type roles. (¶7)
- **Level expectations** (how many competencies to cover):
  - Entry (4–6): Problem Solving, Delivery, Learning + 1–2 culture-fit ones.
  - Mid (5–7): add Initiative, Trust & Conflict, plus Customer Focus or Innovation.
  - Senior+ (7–10): must include Strategic Leadership and Developing Others, plus Innovation and whatever else the target company values. (¶9–11)

**Mining experiences for stories** (§"Mining Your Experiences for Story-Worthy Moments")
- Reframe: a single multi-month project isn't "one story" — it contains several distinct behavioral moments (a tough early decision, a team split you helped resolve, a novel technical approach, an unexpected setback you absorbed, someone you mentored). Each moment = its own story about a different competency, even from the same project. (¶2–4, illustrated with a database-migration example)

**Building your story bank** (§"Building Your Story Bank")
- Start from significant recent experiences (major projects, hard situations, role transitions).
- Look inside each for moments where you: faced a direction-changing decision; solved a problem others couldn't; got a group to align; delivered despite a major obstacle; learned something that changed how you work; stepped up beyond your defined role. (¶3, bullet list)
- Develop each moment as its own story (not the whole project narrative) — see Ch.3 (HSS) for structure. (¶4–5)
- Practice natural delivery — stories should start a conversation, not play back as a memorized monologue. (¶7)

**Ensuring Story Strength → The Four Dimensions** (§"The Four Dimensions" — introduced here, fully defined in Ch.2 below)
- Every story signals: **Scope, Contribution, Impact, Difficulty.** Your story portfolio needs to hit your target level across all four, not just one. (full definitions live in page 3 below)

**Matching Company Values** (§"Matching Company Values")
- Research beyond the careers page: employee blogs, tech talks, published interview guides — figure out what a company's stated values actually mean in practice. (§"Research Their Values")
- Map your existing stories against those values to find **gaps** (e.g., they say "thinking big," your stories are all incremental). (§"Identify Your Gaps")
- Fill gaps by digging for overlooked experiences (side projects, incidents) or by re-emphasizing a different angle of an existing story — same project, different lens (technical depth vs. customer impact vs. cross-team coordination vs. speed). (§"Fill What's Missing")
- Hard rule: only tell **true** stories — you're choosing which real experiences/angles to emphasize, never fabricating. Fabrication risk = instant rejection to career-ending. (¶ near end of section)

**How to use this book** (§"How To Use This Book") — practical routing based on your timeline:
- Interview next week → Ch.3 (HSS) + Ch.4 (Essential Questions) first, then skim Part II at your level, then Ch.14 (delivery).
- Actively interviewing multiple companies → read Part I fully, use Part II as reference per company.
- Planning ahead → read straight through.
- Assessing readiness → start at Ch.2 ("What Companies Are Looking For") to benchmark your level.

## 3. 02 — What Companies Are Looking For
`.../what-companies-are-looking-for`

**Core framing** (¶1–3, intro)
- Companies use behavioral interviews to answer two questions: **(1) Do you fit the role and company? (2) At what level will you be most effective?**
- Get fit wrong → rejected regardless of skill. Get level wrong → down-leveled or rejected as underqualified.

**Understanding Fit** (§"Understanding Fit: Role and Company")
- **Role Fit:** can you handle *this specific position's* working conditions (ambiguity level, pace, coordination demands)?
- **Company Fit:** will your working style/decision-making/values match how this org actually operates (not just surface culture)?

**How companies detect fit** (§"How Companies Detect Fit Through Signals")
- Role Fit signals: comfort with ambiguous requirements, cross-team coordination ability, rapid shipping/iteration — matched to what the role actually demands.
- Company Fit signals: come from the *choices* you describe and how you frame them (e.g., "bias for action," "customer obsession," "radical transparency" type values). The same behavior can read as a strength at one company and a red flag at another. (¶ last, "The same story can send different signals...")

**Common "Mis-Fit" axes** (§"Common “Mis-Fits”") — useful self-check spectrum, none is universally "right":
- Independence vs. Collaboration
- Speed vs. Thoroughness
- Excellence vs. Pragmatism
- Innovation vs. Stability
- Direct vs. Diplomatic
- Data vs. Intuition
- Specialist vs. Generalist
→ Know which side your target company sits on before you pick which stories/framing to lead with.

**The Four Dimensions That Determine Your Level** (§"The Four Dimensions That Determine Your Level")
This is the core evaluation rubric — every story you tell gets read against all four:
- **Scope** — how many people/teams your work affected, and how far out it radiated (self → team → 1 other team → 2+ teams → org-wide), roughly mapping Entry → Mid → Senior → Staff → Principal.
- **Contribution** — precision about "I" vs. "we"; expected to progress from executing assigned work (entry) → owning full solutions (mid) → leading initiatives under ambiguity (senior) → setting cross-team technical direction (staff) → building org-wide capability (principal).
- **Impact** — quantified results connected to business/user outcomes, not just technical metrics; same entry→principal progression in scale of what "improved."
- **Difficulty** — complexity of the problem/constraints/trade-offs handled, not just how good the outcome looked; entry = straightforward-once-understood, up through principal = defining the problem before you can even solve it.
*(Level-by-level story examples in this section are illustrative fluff for calibration — skipped here per your ask; the four dimensions + rough level ladder above is the reusable part.)*

**Self-assessment** (§"Reading and Calibrating Your Own Level" → "Assessing Your Stories" / "Interpreting Your Results")
- Score each of your prepared stories against the four dimensions and target level.
- If most cluster below target: find stronger examples, better articulate scope/impact of what you have, or reconsider the level you're targeting.
- Aim for **2–3 stories solidly at your target level** — mixed levels are normal, but don't lead with weak ones.
- If a specific dimension is weak: Scope → find multi-party examples; Impact → find quantified outcomes; Difficulty → find real constraints/trade-offs; Contribution → isolate what *you* specifically decided/did.

**Common Mismatches to avoid** (§"Common Mismatches") — patterns that cause down-leveling/rejection:
- Junior stories told for senior roles (individual task completion, no mention of influence/teaching/prevention).
- Mid-level stories told for staff roles (single-team ownership when cross-team leadership is required).
- Claiming unearned scope (describing a piece of a larger effort as if you owned the whole thing) — interviewers probe this and it destroys credibility fast.
- All impact, no difficulty (straightforward work dressed up as senior-level problem-solving).
- All difficulty, no impact (deep technical work with no explained business reason — reads as overengineering).
- Unclear contribution ("we" throughout — makes your level impossible to assess).

**Researching what companies actually value** (§"Researching What Companies Really Value")
- **Start with your recruiter** — ask directly: "What should I know about this company's current challenges?" / "What competencies matter most for this role?" / "Can you share any interview prep materials?" Recruiters are incentivized to help you succeed.
- **Mine public info:** engineering blogs, tech talks, open-source choices, technical docs, published postmortems (postmortems = signal the company values learning from failure).
- **Glassdoor/Blind/Reddit:** ignore individual rants, look for *repeated patterns* across multiple posts.
- **Talk to current employees**, but ask specific behavioral questions, not generic culture ones: "When someone gets promoted here, what do they do to earn it?" / "What behaviors get negative feedback?" / "How does the team decide when there's disagreement?" / "What surprised you most about working here?"

**Bottom line** (§"Putting It All Together")
- Fit tells you which experiences/framing will land; level tells you how to position them. Getting both right — not just "any offer" — is the actual goal.

## 4. 03 — High-Signal Storytelling (HSS)
`.../high-signal-storytelling`

**The core problem** (¶1–3, intro)
- Default instinct is to tell a *project* story (chronological, context-heavy). Behavioral interviewers actually need a *behavioral* story (your thinking/decisions/actions). Context-first storytelling buries the signal and gets strong candidates down-leveled or rejected even when the underlying work was solid.

**The HSS Pyramid** (§"The High-Signal Storytelling Pyramid")
Three layers, mapped to how interviewers actually take notes (a one-line summary + bulleted specifics + follow-up notes):
1. **Headline** (peak) — one sentence, prepared in advance.
2. **Behavioral Core** (middle) — ~2 minutes, prepared in advance.
3. **Base** (foundation) — built live, in response to follow-up questions.
- Good structure = fast to deliver, easy for the interviewer to note-take, and adapts to wherever they steer it. (¶ bullet list, "A good story structure does the following")

**The Peak: Headline** (§"The Peak: Your Headline")
- One sentence that could be the interviewer's own note — confirms you understood the question, previews your specific contribution, and "signposts" where the story is going.
- If you have multiple stories that could answer the question, you can offer the interviewer a choice between headlines and let them pick — signals depth of prep and hands them control.
- Pause briefly after delivering it before continuing, so they can redirect if it's not what they wanted.

**The Middle: Behavioral Core** (§"The Middle: Your Behavioral Core")
- 2–3 key moments, each following: minimal context → your thought process → your specific action → the result (with metrics folded in here, not saved for the end).
- What belongs: observation, reasoning, investigation, discovery, decision, action, measurable outcome.
- What doesn't: company history, team org chart, tech-stack justification, meeting play-by-play, what other people tried first.

**The Base: Prepared Depth** (§"The Base: Your Prepared Depth")
- Everything you *don't* volunteer up front but have ready: implementation detail, extended team dynamics, extra context, alternatives considered, deeper lessons — pulled out only by follow-up questions.

**HSS vs. STAR / CARL** (§"How HSS Relates to STAR and CARL")
- STAR's flaw: Situation + Task eat the clock before you reach Action (the actual signal); forcing a "Task" also makes self-initiated/senior stories feel artificial.
- CARL's flaw: mandatory "Learning" ending forces generic insights when impact/metrics would be the stronger close.
- Conversion: Headline pulls from S+A+R; Core expands Action with your thinking; S/T shrink into Base; Result moves into the Core (not the end); Learning moves to Base, ready if asked.

**Signal vs. Noise** (§"Understanding Signal vs. Noise")
- **Signal** = your thoughts, decisions, specific actions, how you worked with others, results/impact — literally what interviewers write down.
- **Noise** = company backstory, team-roster detail, over-justified tech choices, drawn-out timelines.
- Litmus test: *"Would an interviewer actually write this in their notes?"* If not, cut it. (§"Recognizing Noise", closing test)
- **Just-in-time context** (§"Just-In-Time Context"): introduce a fact only at the exact moment it's needed to make a decision make sense — don't front-load setup.

**Red Flags to Avoid** (§"Red Flags to Avoid") — patterns that actively damage candidacy (context-dependent — a positive at one company can be a red flag at another):
- Lying or exaggerating (metrics inflation especially — interviewers check references).
- Blaming others / externalizing failure.
- Vague contribution ("we" throughout, or "I was involved in").
- Showing poor judgment (shortcuts that reveal bad decision-making, not initiative).
- Dismissing team processes (e.g., skipping code review).
- Lacking self-awareness (playing hero in a story where you were actually in the wrong).
- Complaining throughout, even about real problems.

**Staying professional about negative experiences** (§"Staying Professional About Negative Experiences") — five techniques:
- Focus on facts, not emotions.
- Acknowledge the other side's perspective as legitimate.
- Own what was in your control.
- Find the actual learning.
- Keep it brief — spend most of the airtime on your response, not the problem.

**Building your headline** (§"Crafting Your Headline")
- 10–15 seconds; must confirm you understood the question, preview your action/decision, and say why it mattered.
- Mismatched headline-to-level (e.g., entry-level candidate claiming org-wide platform impact) immediately signals you don't understand the role or don't have the experience. (§"Crafting Your Headline", near end)
- Can offer 2 headline options and let the interviewer choose. (§"Using Headlines to Offer Options")

**Building your behavioral core — 3 steps** (§"Building Your Behavioral Core")
1. **Find your actions** — list everything you did, unfiltered.
2. **Select key points** — rank by impact, keep the top **2–3** (fewer = not enough evidence, more = dilutes focus).
3. **Structure each** with the 4-part pattern: minimal context → thought process → your action ("I," not "we") → result.

**Preparing your Base — six categories interviewers pull from** (§"Preparing Your Base"):
1. **Technical Details** — implementation specifics, alternatives considered, trade-offs. Reveal in layers; pause after each layer and let them ask for more.
2. **Working With Others** — how you got buy-in, handled disagreement, worked cross-boundary. Increasingly important at staff/principal (influence without authority).
3. **Context and Background** — why the problem existed, what was tried before, constraints — only surface if asked.
4. **Learning and Growth** — what you'd do differently, patterns you now recognize. Expected at senior+; good to have ready even at entry/mid.
5. **Failure and Recovery** — what went wrong, how you diagnosed it, how you prevented recurrence. Reveals resilience; keep focus on your actions, not blame.
6. **Scale and Metrics** — be ready to go deeper than your headline number. Prefer rough, honest approximations ("cut costs by about 60%") over suspiciously precise recalled figures.

## 5. 04 — The Essential Questions
`.../the-essential-questions`
*(Six questions asked in nearly every interview — shorter/more direct than competency stories, except the "proud of a project" question, which uses full HSS.)*

### Q1 — "Tell Me About Yourself"
**Tests:** first impression — communication, technical background, professional maturity. Sets the tone for the rest of the interview. (§Q1 ¶1)

**Framework — "Power of Three":**
1. Professional Identity & Career Summary (2–3 sentences: role + specialization + brief trajectory, max 2–3 companies — trajectory, not a full history).
2. Three Defining Elements — pick 3 from: technical specialties, major accomplishments, working-style traits, or problems you're passionate about.
3. Connection to Role — explicitly tie the three elements to *this* position.

**Common pitfalls:** starting with unrelated bio; unrelated info; walking through resume chronologically; listing tech stack with no context; talking past 2 minutes; being too modest or overselling; diving into technical detail.

**Pro tips:** write + rehearse until natural (avoid corporate-speak); reuse Ch.14's "Progressive Practice"; adjust emphasis for recruiter vs. technical interviewer; optional brief personal note at the end if relevant; lead with recent/relevant achievements.

---

### Q2 — "Tell Me About a Project You're Proud Of / Most Complex / Most Technically Challenging"
**Tests:** lets you choose the story that shows you at your best — the one variation of this question where you pick the topic. (§Q2 ¶1–2)

**Approach:** pull from your Part-II competency stories (Initiative / Delivery / Problem-Solving / Innovation / Strategic Leadership) based on which variant is asked, and structure with **HSS** (headline → behavioral core → let the interviewer's follow-ups pull from your base).

**If nothing comes to mind, reframe using these angles:**
- Projects that required real learning (the climb, not just the current familiarity).
- "Boring" projects with hidden complexity (scale, optimization, reliability under the hood).
- Projects that *prevented* problems (monitoring/preventive engineering).
- Small-scope, big-impact work (don't discount by size — look at the impact).

**Common pitfalls:** spending the core on background instead of decisions/actions; not separating "I" from "the team"; technical complexity with no business impact stated; picking a project where your role is unclear; front-loading implementation detail that belongs in the base; not prepping the base at all (can't sustain follow-ups).

**Pro tips:** prep one strong headline per variant (proud/complex/challenging) — core can stay mostly the same; cap the core at ~2 minutes; be honest about what was actually hard (authenticity reads better than false ease).

---

### Q3 — "Why This Company / Role?"
**Tests:** whether you've done real homework and have genuine motivation — used to gauge offer-acceptance likelihood and retention. Especially weighted in final rounds. (§Q3 ¶1–2)

**Investment level:** ~1–2 hours of research once an interview is actually scheduled (not for every company you apply to broadly).

**Research the team, not just the company** — because most roles are team-specific hires:
- LinkedIn on your interviewers/team.
- Team-tagged blog posts / GitHub ownership / release notes.
- Immerse in the team's actual product/domain.
- Recent launches, roadmap, conference talks by the team.
- If you can't find the team → ask the recruiter directly which team/org you'd join.
- Exception: some companies (historically Google/Meta-style) hire into a general pool and match teams later — in that case research the company/domain broadly instead.

**Research depth by team type:**
- Product teams → become a power user of the product itself.
- Backend/Infra/Platform → engineering blogs, postmortems, OSS, tech-stack signals in job postings.
- B2B → sign up for a trial, do competitive analysis.
- Plus: GitHub activity, published talks, Hacker News threads, Glassdoor (read between lines), LinkedIn alumni paths.

**Know your own priorities first** (so research is targeted, not generic): technical growth, impact type (user-facing vs. foundational), culture/practices, and domain interest. (§"What Do You Want?")

**Answer structure:** Specific Research Insight → Your Relevant Experience → Mutual Value (what you'd bring *and* what excites you — not just what you'd gain).

**Common pitfalls:** surface-level "About Us" research only; generic gushing praise; only talking about what you'd gain; reciting the job description back; mentioning perks (food, remote, commute); signaling desperation ("I really need this job"); asking questions that only get marketing answers.

**Pro tips:** team-specific research differentiates you immediately; ask the recruiter which team if unknown; research interviewers on LinkedIn; cite specific technical decisions, not just the general stack; connect multiple sources together in your questions; be genuinely curious (forced enthusiasm shows); tailor answer to audience (technical interviewer vs. hiring manager); show you understand their business constraints.

### Q4 — "Do You Have Any Questions for Me?"
**Tests:** whether you're evaluating them too — never answer "no." (§Q4 ¶1–2)

**Tailor questions to who's asking:**
- **Recruiter/HR** — the only audience for comp, benefits, process/timeline, salary range, performance-eval, team-structure questions.
- **Technical team members** — feature-work vs. tech-debt ratio; what support/resources they get; how technical decisions get made (autonomy vs. micromanagement signal); what they'd change; ask about a recent launch and what they learned from it.
- **Hiring managers** — what you'd be assigned immediately (reveals priorities); how long the role's been open (urgency/red-flag signal); onboarding/ramp process; team tenure (stability signal, indirect).
- **Cross-functional partners (PM/design/data)** — how their team collaborates with eng (partnership vs. order-taking); roadmap/prioritization process; what's been harder than expected; specific product-behavior questions if you've used it.

**Avoid:** questions answerable from the About page or already covered; comp/benefit questions to non-recruiters; desperation questions ("How did I do?" / "How many other candidates?"); questions that only get you a marketing answer ("What's great about working here?"); distant hypotheticals ("Where do you see the company in 10 years?").

**Pro tips:** prep 5–7 questions (some get answered along the way); specific questions get specific/honest answers, vague ones get vague answers; escalate depth by interview stage — early rounds = role/team, later = challenges/direction, final = contribution-focused.

### Q5 — "What's Your Biggest Weakness?"
**Tests:** self-awareness, honesty, and active improvement — not the weakness itself. (§Q5 ¶1)

**Three-part structure:**
1. **Select a real weakness** — honest, non-disqualifying for *this* role (ideally one you've actually gotten feedback on). Weigh it against the role's core requirements (e.g., delegation matters far more for a senior/lead role than a junior IC role).
2. **Specific context** — one real example, 1–2 sentences max, just enough to prove it's real.
3. **Active improvement** — the bulk of the answer (~2–3x longer than the weakness description): concrete behavioral changes, seeking help/mentorship, building a system/checklist, skill development, and how you're measuring progress. Recent, specific progress lands best.

**"Strength-as-weakness" is valid only if it genuinely caused a real problem** (not just a disguised brag) — e.g., thoroughness tipping into over-investment on marginal gains. A "weakness" with no real cost attached reads as fake.

**Four failure patterns to avoid:**
- The Humblebrag ("I work too hard").
- The Fake/Cliché Weakness ("I'm a perfectionist" with no specifics).
- The Vague Weakness With No Action ("I struggle with communication sometimes").
- The Disqualifying Weakness (a weakness core to the job itself — raises "why are you applying" questions).

**If nothing comes to mind, mine these sources:**
- Recent performance-review feedback patterns.
- Feedback you've heard from *multiple* people independently.
- A specific mistake and its root cause.
- A skill colleagues have that doesn't come naturally to you.
- Moments you were frustrated with your own performance.

**Pro tips:** pick something you're actively working on; show self-awareness without self-deprecation; the improvement *journey* is the point, not the starting weakness; make sure it can't disqualify you for the role; bring a recent-progress data point.

### Q6 — "Why Are You Looking? / Why Did You Leave? / Resume Gaps"
**Tests:** deliberateness of your move and screens for red flags — fleeing self-created problems, badmouthing past employers, resume instability patterns, or a story that doesn't add up. (§Q6 ¶1)

**Core principle: you don't owe specifics.** "It wasn't a good fit" is sufficient and true — you don't need to disclose being fired, PIP'd, or asked to leave. If you made it to the interview, whatever gap/pattern is on your resume already wasn't disqualifying — don't over-apologize or over-explain.

**Answer shape:** brief context (if any) → what you're looking for → why *this* role fits it. Target 30 seconds–1 minute.

- **Voluntary departure:** frame toward what you're moving *toward* (growth, learning, impact, company-stage change, stronger technical culture) — never toward what you're fleeing. Keep any description of your current employer neutral-to-positive.
- **Layoff:** not your fault, no shame, very common in tech — state it factually with brief context, then pivot immediately to what you're seeking.
- **Bad-fit ending:** "It wasn't a good fit," optionally one sentence on the lesson learned if pressed — don't volunteer more than that.
- **Resume gaps** (layoff job-search, caregiving, health): one to two neutral sentences, then pivot to readiness/excitement — same non-apologetic principle.
- **One short tenure:** easy — "wasn't a good fit" + a one-line lesson.
- **Multiple short tenures:** needs a bit more — a *different*, specific reason per role (to show these weren't the same mistake repeated), then state clearly that you're deliberately seeking a long-term fit now.

**Pro tips:** keep the story identical across every interviewer at the same company (inconsistency itself is a red flag); practice delivering the short version without the urge to elaborate; for genuinely difficult/emotional situations, consider working through it with a therapist first so you can discuss it neutrally in the room.

## Cross-Cutting Threads (show up in 3+ pages)
- **The "I" vs. "we" line** is the single most repeated tell for assessing your actual level/contribution — shows up in Roadmap, What Companies, and HSS.
- **Fabrication is explicitly, repeatedly flagged as a losing strategy** — not just an ethics aside, but because follow-up questions are specifically designed to expose overstated scope/impact.
- **Level-matching your story to your target role** (not just having "good" stories) is treated as equally important to story quality itself — this is the throughline connecting Roadmap's "Story Strength" pillar, What Companies' Four Dimensions, and HSS's headline-calibration point.
